Welcome to Internet History Course

My name is Charles Severance,
and I’m a faculty member
at the University of Michigan
School of Information.

I’ve been working on this material,
in one form or another,
for nearly 30 years.

For me, it really goes back
to the mid-1990s—

when the Internet
was just getting started.

At that time,
I had the good fortune
to work with some friends—

John Liskey,
Amy Leahy,
and Richard Wiggins—

and we produced a television show
about the Internet.

We didn’t know
what it was going to become.

So we went around filming people,
capturing ideas,

and building what turned into
an informal oral history
of the early Internet.

Around that same time,
I was also building technology
for teaching on the Internet.

In fact,
I was one of the first people
to stream an entire semester online.

So early
that I couldn’t even stream video—

just audio,
and slides that flipped
every few minutes.

And a somewhat unsettling
still image of me
in the corner.

In 1999,
I moved to the University of Michigan

and built a more advanced
lecture capture system—

something that could record video,
screens,
and annotations.

Back then,
everything had “2000” in the name,

so I called it
Clipboard 2000.

Later,
around 2010,

I began traveling
with a small camera kit—

often adding a few days
to business trips—

to interview people
who had played key roles
in the development of the Internet.

Those interviews helped complete
a collection of voices and stories

from across the history
of computing and networking.

All of that came together
in the original version
of this course—

which I created
to share both the history
and the technology
of the Internet.

This version of the course
is an updated
and refined version
of that work.

The idea behind this course
is simple:

You can’t really understand technology
without understanding its history.

Technology doesn’t exist
in a vacuum.

It evolves over time,

shaped by people,
constraints,
and decisions—

that often seemed small
at the time—

but turned out
to matter a lot.

So this course is designed
to give you a way in—

especially if you come
from a non-technical background.

This material originally grew
out of a course I taught

between 2009 and 2017.

At the time,
it was part of a larger
programming course.

Right after the midterm,

I would give students
a break from Python—

and teach a couple of weeks
of Internet history
and technology.

This course is that
“break from Python”—

expanded
and refined.

One practical note:

This course can have
a large number of students,

so I’m not always able
to respond
to every question directly.

But I do pay attention.

If something is broken,
I want to know.

Let me know in the question
and answer section
of the course.

So that’s a bit
of the backstory.

Now let’s get started.

And we’ll begin
at the beginning—

at Bletchley Park.
